What the experiments taught us

Three findings we did not expect

1

A consistency test caught a bug the error could not

Optical flow gets noisier with altitude; our filter assumed constant noise. Position error looked fine at 0.42 m — but NIS was 10.15 when it should be 2. The filter was accurate and still wrong about its own confidence.

Scaling R with estimated altitude → NIS 1.95, RMSE unchanged.

2

The covariance admits what it cannot know

No sensor observes horizontal position, so σx and σy grow without bound while σz stays pinned by the altimeter.

That is observability visible in the mathematics — not a bug.

3

Adding a sensor made it worse

A 2.5 m GPS hurt over 60 s (0.434 → 0.750 m): optical flow was already better than GPS. It only starts helping past ~300 s, once drift exceeds GPS noise.

More sensors is not automatically better.

A filter can be accurate and still be dishonest about how accurate it is.

These three findings are what turn a textbook exercise into a real investigation. The first is the most important lesson: we validated with NEES and NIS, which check whether the filter's claimed uncertainty matches its actual error. The position error looked perfectly healthy, but NIS was five times what it should be — the filter was over-confident because we had told it the optical flow noise was constant when in reality it grows with altitude. Fixing it barely changed the RMSE but made the filter honest. If you only ever look at RMSE you would never have found this. Second, the uncertainty in x and y grows forever because nothing measures them, and the graph shows exactly that — the mathematics is telling us the truth about observability. Third, the counter-intuitive one: adding a GPS actually made things worse over a short flight, because optical flow was already more accurate than a consumer GPS. Fusion only helps when the new sensor beats what you already have.
