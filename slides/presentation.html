Conclusions

What we showed

  • Unit 4’s algorithms work on continuous states.
  • Treating wind as an answer keeps it linear.
  • Three weak sensors beat one, by 238×.
  • We found what nothing measures.

Limits

  • Gentle flight only. Aerobatics need an EKF.
  • The IMU reports thrust, not drag.
  • Wind is the same everywhere on the drone.
  • The trajectory is simulated.

Being accurate and knowing it are different things.
Only a consistency test tells you which you have.

Speaker notes

  1. cover — Our problem: a drone flying in gusty wind has to know where it is and how fast it is going. It carries an IMU, an altimeter and an optical-flow camera. All three are noisy, they run at different rates, and none of them measures the wind that is pushing the drone around. We solve it with a Linear Dynamical System and a Kalman filter — which, as we will show, is the same machinery as the HMM material from this unit, moved from discrete states to continuous ones. The headline result is that the filter is 238 times more accurate than simply integrating the accelerometer.
  2. problem — Each sensor is individually inadequate. The IMU gives acceleration, and integrating it twice turns a small bias into a huge position error — it drifted over a hundred metres in our one-minute flight. The altimeter gives height and nothing about horizontal position. Optical flow gives horizontal velocity but degrades with altitude, because there are fewer ground features to track — remember that detail, it comes back later as the error we caught. On top of all that, the wind is never measured by anything, and it is the dominant disturbance. They also run at three different rates, so they cannot simply be lined up and compared. Our job is to fuse three partial, noisy, differently-timed signals into one consistent estimate, and to recover the wind as a bonus.
  3. bridge — This is the slide that ties the project to the syllabus, so spend time here. The point is that we are not doing something separate from HMMs — we are doing the same thing with a continuous state space. Instead of hidden states like Rainy and Sunny, our hidden state is a vector of nine real numbers. The forward algorithm becomes the Kalman filter. Forward-backward becomes the RTS smoother. Baum-Welch becomes EM for the noise covariances. Even the scaling factors have a counterpart: in the HMM we normalise alpha to stop underflow, and here we accumulate log-likelihood through the innovation covariance for exactly the same reason. And Viterbi: because everything is Gaussian and therefore has a single peak, the most likely path is just the sequence of smoothed means, so Viterbi and forward-backward agree here — which is not true for a discrete HMM.
  4. model — One idea on this slide. Most treatments regard wind as noise to be tolerated. We list it as a thing to be estimated, next to position and velocity, so the filter must work it out. Two details make that possible. First, air resistance depends on the drone's speed through the air, not over the ground. That is what links wind into the equations — a gust changes how the aircraft is pushed, and the filter notices the mismatch between prediction and measurement. Model drag against ground speed instead and the wind becomes unrecoverable in principle, not merely hard. Second, we let gusts fade gradually rather than flicker randomly, because real turbulence lasts seconds. People assume that realism forces an extended Kalman filter, but it does not: this stays linear, so the ordinary filter is still exactly optimal. Realism at no cost.
  5. kalman — Strip away the mathematics and the Kalman filter is two steps repeated. Predict: use physics to say where the drone should be a moment later. That guess always becomes less certain, because the world is not perfectly predictable. Correct: when a sensor reports, compare it with the prediction. The gap is called the innovation — more plainly, the surprise. Shift the guess toward the reading: a long way if that sensor is trustworthy, hardly at all if it is noisy, and the certainty improves. The important part is that the filter works out how far to move entirely by itself, from the noise levels. There are no hand-tuned weights anywhere. And because predicting is separate from correcting, sensors running at different rates need no special treatment: predict every step, correct only when something actually reports.
  6. why — We expect to be asked this, so here it is directly. The honest reason is that we built the model to be linear with Gaussian noise, and for that exact case there is a theorem: the Kalman filter is optimal. Not merely good or convenient — optimal. Nothing can beat it. So anything more sophisticated would be solving a harder problem than we actually have. Briefly down the list: averaging is meaningless because the sensors measure different quantities. A complementary filter takes two sensors with one hand-tuned weight and reports no uncertainty. Extended and unscented filters exist to handle non-linear models, and we went out of our way to keep ours linear, so they would only add approximation error. A particle filter costs roughly a hundred times more computation and only earns it when the uncertainty has several peaks, which ours does not. A discrete HMM cannot represent continuous position without millions of bins. And a neural network would need a large labelled dataset, report no uncertainty, and be unable to explain itself — and as our NIS finding shows, knowing the uncertainty is the entire point.
  7. smoother — Two more unit-4 algorithms, both delivering. The RTS smoother is the backward half of forward-backward. The filter is causal - at any moment it only knows the past - but once the flight is over we know everything, so we sweep backwards and let later evidence improve earlier estimates. That is gamma proportional to alpha times beta, and it cut our velocity error by a factor of three and a half. Then EM: we deliberately started the filter believing the sensors were three times noisier than they really are, and it recovered the altimeter noise to within one per cent. The log-likelihood increased at every iteration, which is EM's theoretical guarantee showing up in practice. Worth stressing: nobody fed the filter the true noise values. It worked them out from the data alone.
  8. realdata — We wanted to avoid the usual weakness of a simulation project, which is that every number is chosen by the author. So we measured two of them. First, the accelerometer noise comes from parsing a real PX4 flight log from an actual quadrotor - we measure the noise and the uncorrected bias from the log rather than assuming a value. Second, the turbulence: we pull a week of real hourly wind and gust observations from the Open-Meteo archive, find the gustiest hour, and use the gap between mean wind and gust to set the turbulence intensity. That hour had nearly ten metre per second gusts, which is genuinely rough flying. Everything then goes into a real SQLite database with a proper schema, about thirty-six thousand rows, and the results table on the next slide is produced by a SQL join against the ground truth, not by printing variables. We are honest that the trajectory itself is simulated; what is real is the noise and the weather driving it.
  9. results — Here is the payoff. On the left, the true path and the Kalman estimate are indistinguishable across a long wind-blown arc, while the IMU-only track stays near the start - it never sees the wind, so it has no idea the drone has been blown a hundred metres downwind. Position error drops from a hundred and three metres to forty-three centimetres, a factor of two hundred and thirty-eight. The smoother tightens that to thirty-two centimetres and cuts velocity error by more than three times. The result we are proudest of is the last box: we estimate the wind to about two metres per second even though not one sensor on the aircraft measures wind. It is recovered purely from the gap between what the physics predicted and what the sensors reported.
  10. findings — These three findings turn a textbook exercise into a real investigation. The first is the most important. We validated with NEES and NIS, which check whether the filter's claimed uncertainty matches its actual error. The position error looked perfectly healthy at forty-three centimetres, but NIS came out at ten when it should be two - the filter was five times over-confident, because we had told it the optical flow noise was constant when in reality it grows with altitude. Scaling that noise with the estimated altitude fixed the consistency and barely moved the accuracy at all. If you only ever look at error, you never find this. Second, the uncertainty in sideways position grows forever because no sensor observes it, while height stays pinned by the altimeter - the mathematics telling us the truth about observability. Third, the counter-intuitive one: adding a GPS made things worse over a short flight, because optical flow was already more accurate than a consumer GPS. Fusion only helps when the new sensor beats what you already have.
  11. conclusion — To close. We set out to estimate a drone's position and velocity from three inadequate sensors in turbulent wind, and we did it with the Unit 4 toolkit moved from discrete states to continuous ones. The headline numbers are a 238 times improvement over dead reckoning and the recovery of wind, which nothing on the aircraft measures. We are explicit about the limits: linear drag and gentle flight, a simplified IMU channel, uniform wind, and a simulated trajectory driven by real measured noise and real measured gusts. If we had to keep one lesson it is that being accurate and knowing you are accurate are different properties. Our filter had an excellent error while being badly over-confident, and only the NIS consistency test revealed it. Thank you - happy to take questions.
